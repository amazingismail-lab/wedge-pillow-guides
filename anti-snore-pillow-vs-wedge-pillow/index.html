Anti-Snore Pillow vs Wedge Pillow: Which Fits Your Snoring?

By James Park · November 2026

If you're weighing an anti snore pillow vs wedge pillow, the real question is which part of your body needs to move. An anti-snore pillow changes where your head and neck sit; a wedge pillow lifts your entire upper body on an angled foam base. Which one may help often comes down to whether your snoring happens mostly when you're flat on your back.

What exactly is an anti-snore pillow?

"Anti-snore pillow" is a label covering several different shapes, not one product. It usually means a contoured neck pillow that tilts the chin up slightly, a head-positioning pillow with a raised centre that rolls the head to one side, or a powered pillow that inflates to nudge the head sideways when it senses snoring. Because these designs work differently, two pillows sold under the same label can behave nothing alike.

What is a wedge pillow and how is it different?

A wedge pillow is a firm foam ramp that raises the head, neck, and torso together, instead of turning or propping just the head. The NHS snoring page lists "a special pillow or bed wedge" in the same breath, which is part of why the two get compared. For a fuller breakdown of every shape sold under this label, see our anti snore pillow vs wedge pillow comparison guide, which tables out who each design suits.

Does research show anti-snore pillows reduce snoring?

Results are mixed and depend heavily on the specific pillow tested. A 2004 randomised, blinded trial of 40 snorers found no measurable benefit from a head-positioning pillow compared with no aid at all. A 2017 crossover trial of 22 snorers without sleep apnoea found a lower snoring index when the pillow was active. A 2015 study of 25 adults saw the median snoring rate drop from 218 to 115 events per hour, though the effect wasn't significant in participants who were overweight.

Tip

Give any new pillow about a week before judging it. In the 2015 study, normal-weight participants also slept roughly an hour less on the positioning pillow than on their usual one, so early nights may not reflect how it settles.

Does research show wedge pillows reduce snoring?

Incline studies suggest a modest effect that depends on angle. A 2022 home study of 25 self-reported snorers found a 7% relative drop in snoring duration at a 12° incline over four weeks. A 2022 airway imaging study of 45 people with sleep apnoea found less soft-palate collapse at 30°, but no change at the base of the tongue. A typical sleeping wedge, such as the Aeris Wedge Pillow, sits closer to 18–19°, between those two tested angles.

Does sleep position matter more than the pillow?

For many people without sleep apnoea, position alone may matter more than the product. A 2003 study of 72 habitual snorers found that those without apnoea generally snored less lying on their side, while people with sleep apnoea often didn't improve much even on their side. That gives both pillow types a shared target: snoring that's worse specifically when lying flat on your back.

How do I choose between the two?

Start with where your snoring happens and how you prefer to sleep. If you only snore on your back and can tolerate sleeping on an incline, a wedge pillow changes more of your airway at once; if you'd rather stay flat, a contoured or positioning pillow keeps that posture while adjusting the head. Shared-bed space, and whether you also want elevation for reflux or congestion, can tip the choice further. More comparisons like this are collected at wedgepillows.info.

Can I combine an anti-snore pillow with a wedge pillow?

Yes, but stacking a thick pillow on top of a wedge can bend your neck forward and work against the open airway both are meant to support. If you add anything on top of the slope, a thin, flat pillow keeps your neck roughly in line with the incline rather than folding it toward your chest.

When should snoring prompt a doctor visit?

Persistent snoring alongside gasping, choking, or pauses in breathing may be worth raising with a doctor rather than solving with a pillow alone. The NHS specifically advises seeing a GP for daytime sleepiness, breathing that stops and starts during sleep, or snoring that doesn't improve with lifestyle changes — neither pillow type treats sleep apnoea itself.

Frequently asked questions

Is a wedge pillow considered an anti-snore pillow?

It's often marketed alongside them, and the NHS lists a bed wedge next to "a special pillow" for snoring. The difference is what moves: anti-snore pillows reposition the head and neck, while a wedge pillow raises the whole upper body. Both are generally aimed at snoring that's worse specifically when lying flat on your back.

Do anti-snore pillows actually reduce snoring?

Evidence is inconsistent. One randomised trial of 40 snorers found no benefit over no aid at all, while a smaller crossover trial of 22 snorers found a measurable drop in snoring index with an active positioning pillow. Results likely depend on the specific design and on why a person snores in the first place.

Which option suits back sleepers better?

Both target back-sleeping snoring, just differently. A positioning pillow tries to turn the head to one side, while a wedge pillow lets you stay on your back with the airway raised. If an incline feels uncomfortable, a flat positioning pillow may be the easier first test.

Can these pillows cause discomfort or poor sleep?

Some people report mild discomfort when adjusting. In one study, participants slept about an hour less on a head-positioning pillow during the trial period compared with their usual pillow, though most reported no or only mild discomfort. Trying a pillow for a full week may give a more reliable sense of fit.

What incline angle has research tested for wedge pillows?

Published studies have tested angles from about 12° to 30°. A 12° incline produced a modest reduction in snoring duration at home, while 30° reduced airway collapse at the soft palate in a clinical setting. Common sleeping wedges tend to sit around 18–19°, within that tested range.
