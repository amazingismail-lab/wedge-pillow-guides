Stacked Pillows vs Wedge Pillow: Does the Pile Really Work?

By James Park · September 2026

People with reflux, snoring, or a stuffy nose often reach for the pillows already on the bed before buying anything new. That's a reasonable first move, but it changes what actually gets lifted. Here's what a stack does differently from a foam wedge, why the gap tends to show up by the middle of the night, and when stacking is still a fine choice.

What's the real difference between stacked pillows and a wedge pillow?

A pillow stack lifts the head; a wedge lifts the head, neck, shoulders, and upper back on one continuous slope. Reflux and airway symptoms respond to where the chest sits relative to the stomach, not to how high the skull is propped, so a lift that starts at the neck does much less than one that starts under the shoulder blades. Speaking to Cleveland Clinic, gastroenterologist Scott Gabbard, MD, says regular pillows only elevate the head, which isn't enough to prevent acid reflux — a wedge, by contrast, raises the whole torso. Does the research actually support raising your torso?

The trials cited in reflux guidelines elevated the body with bed blocks or a wedge, not with a pile of pillows. A 2021 systematic review pooled five studies covering 228 patients, all of which raised the head end of the body by roughly 20 cm. A separate 2021 crossover trial used a solid 20 cm wedge angled at about 20 degrees. The American College of Gastroenterology's 2022 guideline suggests head-of-bed elevation for nighttime symptoms, but rates it a conditional recommendation based on low-quality evidence — a reasonable thing to try, not a guaranteed fix.

Why does a pillow stack lose its lift overnight?

Because the pillows compress and slide, and neither problem affects a solid wedge. Soft fill is designed to give under weight, so pillows that measure a certain height at bedtime are shorter after a few hours of pressure, and separate pillows on a smooth sheet drift apart every time you turn over. Mattress Clarity's stacking guide warns that a DIY stack "can be prone to shifting and unevenness" as the night goes on. A foam wedge has no seams to separate and a firm core that resists packing down, so its height at 2 a.m. is close to what it was at bedtime.

Tip

Test it yourself: measure your stack's height at the top edge before you lie down, then again if you wake in the night. A stack that's lost a third of its height has lost most of its effect too.

Can stacked pillows cause neck pain?

They can, because raising only the head bends the neck forward. Mattress Clarity itself notes that lifting the head alone "may also cause undue stress on your neck." A wedge avoids this by starting the incline lower, under the shoulder blades, so the spine stays closer to straight. If you already have a neck or back condition, it may be worth checking with a physical therapist or doctor before sleeping elevated at all.

When is stacking pillows still a fine option?

Stacking works well as a one-night test, for travel, or for a mild head lift that isn't meant to raise the torso. If you're not sure elevation will help your reflux or snoring, a free trial with pillows you already own can tell you within a night or two whether it's worth buying foam. A wedge doesn't fit in a carry-on either, so a stack is often the only option away from home.

Note

Puffy's guide recommends two or three firm pillows and avoiding down or feather fill, which "compress unevenly and lose elevation quickly."

How do you build a stack that holds up better?

Use firm pillows, place the lowest one under the shoulder blades, and step the next one up so the slope is gradual rather than a single tall pile under the head. Add a pillow under the knees to stop your body sliding down through the night. Mattress Clarity aims for 6 to 8 inches of head elevation with the torso off the mattress — a target that's genuinely hard to hold with pillows and is closer to what a wedge, such as the Aeris Wedge Pillow, is already cut to.

When should you switch from a stack to a wedge?

Switch once the stack has done its job as a test and you know elevation helps. Common signs include waking with a stiff neck, rebuilding the pile more than once a night, or noticing the relief fades after the first hour. If reflux, snoring, or breathing symptoms persist despite consistent elevation, it's worth talking to a doctor rather than adjusting pillows further. Frequently asked questions

Can stacked pillows replace a wedge pillow for acid reflux?

For a short test, yes — a stack can tell you whether elevation helps at all. For nightly use it's less reliable, since it compresses and slides while the trials behind reflux guidance used a steady ~20 cm lift from a wedge or bed blocks. If a stack helps, that's usually a sign a wedge will hold the effect more consistently through the night.

How many pillows equal one wedge pillow?

Roughly three firm pillows, stepped from the shoulder blades upward, come closest to a low wedge at bedtime. They don't equal it by morning, though, since a stack loses height as the fill compresses and the pillows separate, while a solid wedge keeps its shape.

Why might stacked pillows make reflux feel worse for some people?

Because they can lift the head without the torso, bending the body at the neck and waist instead of creating one straight incline. That fold-point is what several stacking guides warn against, and it's the opposite of the flatter angle used in reflux trials.

Is a wedge better than pillows for snoring?

A wedge tends to hold the head and chest at a steady angle all night, which supports open airways, while a stack's angle changes as it compresses. Snoring that's persistent or paired with gasping or daytime fatigue may point to sleep apnea, which needs a diagnosis from a sleep physician rather than a pillow adjustment.

What kind of pillows should I stack if I don't have a wedge yet?

Firm ones — memory foam or dense fiber fill — hold their height far longer than down or feather. Place the lowest pillow under the shoulder blades, step the next one up gradually, and add a pillow under the knees to keep your body from sliding down the stack overnight.
