How to Sleep on Your Side on a Wedge Pillow

By James Park · September 2026

Can you sleep on your side on a wedge pillow?

Yes, though wedges are built mainly for the back, so side sleeping takes a bit of adjustment to work comfortably. The slope still supports your torso, but your shoulder becomes a pivot point instead of a flat surface, and the hips can twist or slide if nothing holds them in place. Get the shoulder position, hip support, and head-height right and the side position can hold through the night; skip any one of those and the lower back is usually what pays for it by morning. Our full guide to how to sleep on your side on a wedge pillow walks through the setup step by step.

Which side should you sleep on for reflux?

The left side is the one with research behind it. In a 2015 randomized crossover trial, 20 healthy volunteers went through six-hour test periods in four positions — an inclined device left-side down, the same device right-side down, a standard wedge in any position, and flat on a bed — and esophageal acid exposure came out lowest left-side down on the device and highest right-side down on it. A 2023 systematic review pooling two studies found left-side sleeping cut acid exposure time by roughly 2 percentage points compared with the right side, and about 2.7 points compared with lying flat on the back. The American College of Gastroenterology's 2022 guideline lists sleeping on the left side among lifestyle measures with evidence of improvement, and treats head-of-bed elevation as a conditional suggestion for night-time symptoms. If snoring or congestion is the reason for the wedge instead, either side may work — pick whichever one lets you actually stay asleep.

Why does side sleeping on a wedge cause back pain?

Usually because of the shoulder, not the incline itself. On your back, a wedge's slope supports the whole spine evenly; on your side, the lower shoulder has to sink into the foam somewhere along that slope, and if it hangs off the edge or lands at the thin top where there's little cushioning, the spine bends sideways and the lower back compensates. Unsupported hips are the other common cause — side sleeping already lets the upper leg drop forward and roll the pelvis, and an incline pulls that roll further toward the foot of the bed. If pain doesn't ease within a couple of weeks of a corrected setup, it's worth checking in with a doctor or physical therapist, since a wedge is a positioning aid, not a treatment.

Where should your shoulder rest on the slope?

Roughly a third of the way up from the low end, so the foam supports the shoulder from underneath rather than the shoulder bearing weight from the side. Side sleepers generally need more height under the head than back sleepers, since the shoulder holds the head further from the mattress — physical therapist Matthew O'Rourke of Spaulding Rehabilitation Hospital tells Harvard Health that a neck bent for long stretches becomes uncomfortable. A 2025 biomechanics study testing pillow heights for side lying found shoulder width predicted the best-fitting height fairly reliably. On a wedge, the shoulder sinking into the incline closes some of that gap on its own, so a thinner head pillow than usual is typically enough — the Aeris Wedge Pillow is one option built with a wide enough slope for this.

Do you need a pillow between your knees?

It can help more than it would on a flat mattress. The Sleep Foundation notes that a pillow between the knees may improve spinal alignment and ease pressure on the hips and lower back for side sleepers by keeping the body in a more neutral line. On an incline, a knee pillow does double duty: it also anchors the legs on the flat part of the mattress instead of letting them slide down with the rest of the body, which takes some of the load off the torso having to grip the slope alone.

How wide should the wedge be?

Wide enough that your shoulder stays on the foam through a full roll — in practice, that means looking for 60 centimeters or more of width. A narrow wedge works fine for back sleeping, since the body stays centered, but a side sleeper moves and turns, and a narrow slope leaves the shoulder unsupported the moment you shift. Width matters more here than height; a very steep wedge is harder to hold a side position on than a moderate one.

What if you keep sliding down the incline?

Move your hips off the wedge so only your torso is on the slope, and keep your knees bent with a pillow between them. Gravity plus an incline pulls an unsupported body toward the foot of the bed, and anchoring the legs on the flat mattress below the wedge counteracts that pull. If you're still sliding after adjusting hip position and adding a knee pillow, the wedge may simply be steeper than what side sleeping calls for.

Should you switch back to sleeping on your back?

If side sleeping doesn't settle after a couple of weeks of adjustments, sleeping on your back is worth trying instead, since that's the position a wedge is designed around in the first place. The 2015 trial above also noted that people on a plain wedge tended to drift onto their backs overnight regardless of their assigned position, which suggests the body may gravitate there on its own. A wedge under the back with a separate pillow under the knees is a reasonable alternative for the same reflux or snoring goals. For more on wedge setups generally, see wedgepillows.info.
