Wedge Pillow vs Nasal Strips vs Mouth Tape: Which Fits You?

By James Park · November 2026

Snoring starts in different parts of the airway — the nose, the mouth, or the throat — which is why a wedge pillow, nasal strips, and mouth tape each suit a different kind of snorer rather than snoring in general. None of the three treats sleep apnoea. Here's what the research behind each one actually measured, and how to figure out which is worth testing first.

What's actually different between a wedge pillow, nasal strips, and mouth tape?

They act on three different parts of the airway. A wedge pillow raises the head and torso so gravity has less pull on a floppy soft palate when you're on your back; nasal strips hold the outside of the nose open to ease airflow through a blocked or narrow nose; mouth tape holds the lips closed to try to force nose breathing. The NHS snoring page pairs each cause — nose, mouth, or throat — with a different fix, rather than suggesting one product for everyone.

Which one should I try first?

Whichever matches where your snoring seems to start. If you only snore on your back, a wedge pillow is the more logical first test; if your nose feels blocked at night, a nasal strip targets that directly; if your mouth falls open and your nose is clear, mouth tape is aimed at that case specifically — though it carries more risk than the other two.

Tip

Test one aid for at least a week before adding another. Snoring can vary night to night, so a week of notes or recordings tells you more than judging a single night.

What do nasal strips actually change?

They cut how often you snore more than how loud it is. In a placebo-controlled trial of 12 people with chronic rhinitis, strips brought snoring down from 258 to 173 snores per hour, with no measured change in loudness. A broader review of nasal dilators in people with sleep apnoea found no significant change in a snoring index, which suggests strips may help most when a blocked nose, not the throat, is doing most of the work.

Does mouth tape actually work, and is it safe?

The evidence for it is thin, and it can be risky for some people. A 2025 systematic review of 10 studies rated all of them poor quality and concluded the data doesn't support mouth taping as a sound clinical approach for sleep-disordered breathing. A few of the studies reported a lower snoring index, but the same review flagged a real asphyxiation risk if the nose blocks while the mouth is taped shut.

Watch out

Mouth tape assumes your nose can carry your full breathing load all night. If your nose tends to get congested or blocked, taping the mouth shut may not be safe without checking with a doctor first.

Can a wedge pillow stop snoring by itself?

It may reduce it, mainly if you snore on your back, but it likely won't fix tongue-based snoring. A 2022 home study of 25 snorers found a 12° incline produced a 7% relative reduction in snoring duration over four weeks. A separate airway-imaging study at 30° found less collapse at the soft palate and throat walls, but no measurable difference at the base of the tongue, so elevation seems to help one part of the airway and not another. Is the tennis ball trick better than a wedge pillow?

It can work short term, but very few people stick with it. The ball is sewn to the back of a shirt so you can't comfortably lie on your back; in a follow-up of patients who'd been prescribed it, fewer than 10% were still using it around two and a half years later, mostly because it was too uncomfortable. A wedge pillow aims at the same back-sleeping problem but lets you stay on your back instead of forcing you off it.

How do I work out what kind of snorer I am?

Check position, nose, and mouth separately before buying anything. Ask a partner or record yourself: does it only happen on your back? Is your nose blocked or stuffy at night? Does your mouth hang open, or do you wake with a dry mouth? The answers point toward a wedge pillow, nasal strips, or a chin strap/mouth device, respectively — more on sorting this out is on wedgepillows.info.

When is snoring a reason to see a doctor instead of trying another aid?

If it comes with daytime sleepiness, gasping or choking sounds, or pauses in breathing, it's worth getting checked rather than reaching for another product, since persistent or worsening symptoms like these are best discussed with a doctor and can be signs of sleep apnoea, which none of these three aids are designed to treat.

Frequently asked questions

Do nasal strips work better than a wedge pillow for snoring?

It depends on the cause. Nasal strips cut how often people with a blocked or stuffy nose snored in one small trial, but didn't change loudness. A wedge pillow instead targets back-sleeping position and soft-palate collapse. If your nose feels clear and you mainly snore lying on your back, a strip may have little to change.

Is it safe to tape my mouth shut every night?

Not necessarily. A 2025 review of 10 studies found the evidence poor quality overall and warned that taping the mouth shut can pose an asphyxiation risk if the nose becomes blocked during the night. It's worth talking to a doctor before using mouth tape regularly, especially if you get congested often.

Can I combine a wedge pillow, nasal strips, and mouth tape?

A wedge pillow and nasal strips can reasonably be used together since they act on different parts of the airway. Adding mouth tape on top is the riskier combination, because a strip slipping off or a nose blocking overnight could leave less airflow available. Introducing one aid at a time also makes it easier to tell what's actually helping.

How long should I trial each snoring aid before judging it?

Give each one roughly a week with everything else in your routine unchanged, including bedtime and evening meals or drinks. Snoring can vary a lot from night to night, so judging by a single night isn't a reliable test either way, no matter which aid you're trying.

What angle should a wedge pillow be to help with snoring?

Studies on elevation have tested angles from around 12° up to 30°, with benefits showing up differently at each end of that range. A pillow like the Aeris Wedge Pillow, which sits around 18-19°, falls between those tested angles, inside the range the research has looked at so far.
